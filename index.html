How it happens

Nobody gets hacked. A careful person sends the money.

This is business email compromise. It does not break into anything. It looks like an ordinary week in accounts payable, and every step is someone doing their job.

  1. Monday, 9:12

    An email from your supplier

    "We have moved banks. Please use the new details for Friday's payment." The name, the signature and the invoice number are all right.

  2. Monday, 9:40

    Your AP clerk does the careful thing

    She calls the number in the email to check. A friendly voice confirms it. The new account goes into the vendor list.

  3. Friday, 4:05

    The payment run goes to the bank

    Fourteen payments, $286,412.50. One of them, $48,250, goes to the new account. Nothing looks wrong.

  4. Three weeks later

    The real supplier calls

    They were never paid. The account belonged to a scammer, and the money has already moved on.

Why the call-back did not help. The scammer wrote the email, so the scammer chose the phone number in it. Calling that number confirms the scam. A call-back proves something only when it goes to a number you had before the change.

Before and after

Today it is a spreadsheet, a phone call and hope.

Today

  • A change request arrives by email, and someone updates the vendor list.
  • A call-back if there is time, often to the number in the email itself.
  • A spreadsheet of who was checked, when, and by whom. Maybe.
  • The run goes out on Friday because it is Friday.
  • When the bank or the auditor asks for your process, someone writes one.

With Quarter

  • The vendor confirms by logging in to their own bank, or you record a call-back to a number you already had.

  • The written procedure and the evidence are already there when anyone asks.

How it works

Between your payment file and your bank, one more step.

Your team keeps paying the way it does today, from the bank portal, the ERP or the accounting tool. Quarter checks the file before it goes, and gives it back clean.

  1. Upload the run

    The NACHA file or CSV you were about to send, from your bank portal, ERP or accounting tool. Or send it by API.

  2. Every payment is checked

    Is the payee a real vendor, were these bank details confirmed, does the amount look like the ones before it.

  3. A person decides

    Held payments wait with the reason and the evidence. Approve or reject, with a note. Large ones need a second person.

  4. Release a clean file

    Quarter gives the file back without the rejected payments, totals recomputed. You send it to your bank as you do today.

  5. Everything is recorded

    Who checked, who approved, which evidence. Ready for your bank, your auditor or your insurer.

How a vendor proves it

A scammer can write the email and answer the phone. They cannot log in to your supplier's bank.

So that is where the proof comes from. Quarter sends your vendor a link. They log in to their own bank, and if the account is the one on file, in their name, it is confirmed. Payments to it stop being held.

  1. You send the vendor a linkFrom Quarter, to the contact you already had for them. Not to the address that asked for the change.
  2. A code to their company emailQuarter records whether the inbox is on the vendor's own email domain. On its own, that proves little, so it never verifies anything by itself.
  3. They log in to their own bankThrough Plaid, on their bank's own login screen. If the account is the one on file and the owner's name matches the vendor, it is confirmed.

Prefer to call? Record a call-back to a number you held before the change. Quarter keeps who called, which number and where it came from, so the evidence outlives the person who made the call.

How call-backs are recorded

Twenty-two checks before a payment leaves

The questions a careful controller would ask, asked of every payment.

Each one gives its reason in plain words. You choose which ones hold a payment and which only warn. Three are always on: a vendor you blocked, a name on the sanctions list, and a second person for large payments.

  • account_changed_recentlyholdThe bank details changed recently and nobody confirmed them with the vendor.
  • unverified_accountholdThese bank details were never confirmed with the vendor.
  • account_not_on_fileholdThe payment goes to an account that is not the one on file for this vendor.
  • unknown_payeeholdThe payee is not a vendor you have on file.
  • vendor_blockedholdYou blocked this vendor from being paid.
  • duplicate_paymentholdThe same payment appears twice, or was already paid in the last 7 days.
  • shared_accountholdAnother of your vendors is paid at this same account.
  • network_flaggedwarnA business on Quarter reported this account in connection with a payment fraud.
  • sanctions_matchholdThe payee name resembles a name on the OFAC SDN list. Always on
  • second_person_requiredholdAt or above the two-person threshold, or paid by the person who changed the vendor's bank details. Someone else must approve. Always on
  • routing_invalidholdThe routing number is not a valid US bank routing number.
  • iban_invalidholdThe IBAN on an international payment fails its checksum.
  • name_mismatchwarnThe name on the payment does not match the vendor it pays.
  • first_paymentwarnThis is the first payment to this vendor.
  • amount_unusualwarnFar more than this vendor is usually paid.
  • check_number_reusedholdThis check number was already used on the same account.
  • payee_name_alteredwarnThe payee line on a check is close to the vendor's name on file, but not the same.
  • just_under_thresholdwarnA check for just under the amount that needs two approvers.
  • employee_account_matchholdThe account paid, or the vendor's account on file, is on your list of employee bank accounts.
  • vendor_dormant_reactivatedwarnA vendor not paid for a year has new bank details since its last payment.
  • split_below_thresholdwarnPayments to one vendor within 7 days, each under the two-approver amount, together reach it.
  • vendor_new_paid_fastwarnA vendor added in the last 14 days is first paid by the person who added it.

Why now

Your bank, your auditor and your insurer will ask what your process was.

Quarter writes your fraud-monitoring procedure from your own settings, reminds you to review it every year, and keeps the evidence that you followed it. It helps you show a process. It does not make anyone compliant.

The rule now covers every business that sends ACH

Since June 19, 2026, Nacha's rules ask every business that sends ACH payments, of any size, for a risk-based process to spot payments made under false pretenses, such as a scammer posing as a vendor, reviewed at least once a year. The rule does not require checks before release, and call-backs with two-person approval can be enough. What it does require is a process you can show.

Source: Nacha Operating Rules, fraud monitoring phase 2

The email looks right

Impersonation emails used to give themselves away with clumsy writing. Now they arrive in your supplier's tone, with the right invoice number and signature. An email that looks right proves nothing.

Someone will ask what your process was

Your bank, your auditor, or your insurer after a loss. Crime policies with social engineering cover often expect a call-back to a known number on every bank change, and two people on large payments. Quarter keeps the record that you did both.

A note from the founders

Why we are building Quarter

Business email compromise does not break into a bank. It persuades a careful person to send the money themselves. The tools many finance teams have against it are a spreadsheet, a phone call and hope.

We think it can be fixed. A supplier can prove their own bank account in a minute by logging in to their bank. A payment run can be checked line by line before it goes out. And a bank account confirmed by one business could count for every business that pays it.

So that is what we are building, starting with the check every payment run needs. We will always say plainly what is built and what is not. And we will never ask you to trust us with your money, because we never touch it.
